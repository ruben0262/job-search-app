## Your task

Write this person's cover letter for this job: a short letter, written to a person, that makes the reader want to meet them. Their CV proves what they have done. The letter does what a CV cannot: it says why they want this job with this employer, and what they would bring to it, in their own voice. The reader gives it under a minute and is asking three things: why us, why this work, and why you. Answer those plainly and leave the proof and the detail to the CV: the letter is there to make the reader want to meet them, and to open the CV on the way.

Their own cover letters above show how they write. Keep their voice: how formal they are, and how they put things. Most people's letters are weak, generic and full of stock phrases, so keep their voice and leave those phrases behind. Use a true detail from them when it fits this job, but never copy paragraphs written for another job or company. Their CVs and other documents are the record of what they have done.

We add their name and contact details and the design. You write the letter, from the greeting to the signature, in the markup shown further down. Give it no subject line, no address block and no date.

## Plan before you write

Work through these in your thinking first:

1. List the three or four things this employer needs most, in the posting's own words, with any licence, registration or certificate it requires, and note what would make the reader hesitate about this person, such as a move or a requirement their documents do not show.
2. Find why they want this job. What they want from work is in their own cover letters and in what they told us about themselves, often said vaguely and for another employer: what they enjoy in the work, what they want more or less of, the kind of place they want, what they hope to learn or move up to. Where their documents show it, add the direction their work has been taking: what they have moved towards, job after job. Quote those lines. Then find two or three things in this posting that give them that, each particular to the work there: who it serves, what they would work on or with, how the team works, or what it offers someone in their position, such as training, a qualification or a way up. Not its size, reach, awards, values or slogans, and nothing any employer could offer, such as pay, holidays or a friendly team.
3. Choose what they would bring: the two or three things about them that matter most for what this employer needs. Each is a strength, a way of working or a piece of knowledge, with the one thing they did that shows it and what came of it. Quote the line in their documents each comes from. A need their documents do not answer is left alone.

## Rules

### 1. Only what is true

- Every employer, job, date, qualification, tool, skill and number comes from their documents. Never invent or estimate one, and keep each number with what it measured.
- Say each thing exactly as strongly as their documents do. No speciality, rank or label they do not give themselves, such as specialist, main, core, expert or senior; no part of a list as all of it; a result they had with others kept as shared; and nothing the posting asks for claimed as theirs. What they want comes from their own words only.
- Say about the company only what the posting says. Never invent its products, values, news or people.
- Tell their work in your own words rather than repeating the CV's lines, but add no facts: no new numbers, clients, dates or events.

### 2. What the letter says

- **Greeting:** "Dear" and the name of the person hiring, if the posting gives one; otherwise "Dear Hiring Manager,".
- **Opening, two or three sentences:** say why they want this job, then give the one fact about them that makes the reader go on. The reason comes first, as something true and particular about the work there and what it means to them. Then who they are for this job, in a line: what they do, where and for how long, or the result that best stands for them. Name the company and the job in the same breath. The company as people say it, without a legal form, "Group" or a slogan ("Bosch", not "Bosch Group" or "Robert Bosch GmbH"). When a recruiter wrote the posting for a client of theirs, the job is not at the recruiter: say the recruiter is hiring for it ("the … role that Radley James is recruiting for"), and call the client only what the posting calls it. The job by the title the posting gives it, as it reads in a sentence, without hours, contract, place or "m/f/d", and in the language the posting writes it in, even when that is not the letter's. Put that title in `<strong>`, here and wherever else the letter names it, so the reader sees at once which job the letter is for. Never open with the application itself, in any words ("I am writing to apply", "I would like to be considered for"), never restate the posting back to the people who wrote it ("You are looking for…"), never open with an essay about the company, and never with a feeling any applicant could claim ("I was delighted to see…").
- **What they would bring, one paragraph, two if it needs them:** the two or three things you chose, the most important first. Each is a strength or a way of working shown in a sentence or two by one thing they did and what came of it, so that the reader sees the person at work. It is not a walk through the posting's requirements and not their CV in sentences: choose, and leave the rest to the CV. Tell the work itself, in words a reader of this posting will know at once as that part of the job, plain words of your own rather than the posting's phrases. Add nothing to join the two: no sentence about what the job asks ("Your role asks for…", "This role needs…"), and no conclusion their documents do not state ("so I know how to…", "which is what I do every day", "I am used to…"). The fact, well chosen and plainly named, is the connection. Keep the figure that shows the size of each piece of work where their documents give one, and leave the rest to the CV: a run of tools, versions, settings and further figures buries the claim it was meant to prove. Never announce the match as a formula ("That is the same problem your role describes", "You need X, and I have done X", "This is exactly what you ask for"), never quote the posting's requirements back to tick them off, and never call their work the same as this job's when it is not. Name the licences, registrations or certificates this job requires that they hold, and settle what would make the reader hesitate in one plain sentence where their documents can, such as a move they plan. Never apologise for what they lack.
- **Why this job, here, a paragraph of its own, two to four sentences:** this is the heart of the letter. The things you found, each joined to what they said they want or to where their work has been heading, in plain words of their own rather than the posting's: what they would work on or who they would serve there, how the team works, what it would let them learn or become. Say the reason itself, as a fact about the work there and what it gives them. Wanting is said plainly, in words like "I want" and "what I am looking for", and never dressed up. Leave out anything they want that this job does not offer. Never repeat what the posting says about the company itself, and never claim a history with it that their documents do not give, such as having long admired it or used its products.
- **Close, one or two sentences:** confident and about the work, something they want to build, solve or improve there. Never about the process of applying ("I look forward to hearing from you", "Thank you for your consideration", "I would welcome the opportunity"). Then the sign-off ("Kind regards,") and their name.
- **Length:** 220 to 280 words, in paragraphs of two or three sentences, on one page of A4. At least half of it is why they want this job and what they would bring, and no more than half is what they have done.

### 3. How it is written

- **Language:** write in English, whatever language the posting and their documents are in, in a formal register. Spell as the posting does, British or American, and British when the posting is not in English.
- **Voice:** warm, plain and specific, like a capable person writing to someone they would like to work with. Short sentences. Concrete beats impressive.
- Show a quality by what they did, never claim it ("I am good with people"), and never complain about a past job or employer.
- Write words out in full: "I have", not "I've"; "do not", not "don't".
- No colons and no em dashes (—) in sentences: they make a letter read as generated. Rewrite as one plain sentence, or two. "The problem was simple to state: a prompt change could break another case" becomes "The problem was simple to state. A prompt change could fix one case and quietly break another." A list after a colon becomes a sentence: "three services, a scraper, a processor and a scorer".
- Never use these words or phrases, which describe nothing: passionate, results-driven, dynamic, motivated, team player, hard-working, proven track record, leverage, synergy, cutting-edge, seamless, robust, innovative, thrilled, end-to-end, "I am excited to", "I believe I would be a great fit", "the job description".

## Final check

Before you answer, read your letter as the hiring manager will, and fix whatever fails:

1. **The opening.** It says why they want this job, in a way no other job would fit, gives the one fact about them that makes the reader go on, and names the company as people say it and the job by the posting's own title, in `<strong>`. It is not "I am writing to", does not restate the posting, and is not about the company.
2. **Three questions.** A reader who has only this letter knows why this employer, why this work and why this person. What they would bring is two or three things, each shown by work they did, in words that reader knows as their own job, and it is not their CV in sentences. No sentence says what the job asks, announces the match or draws a conclusion their documents do not state. Each piece of work keeps the one figure that shows its size, and no tool, version or setting stays that the reader does not need in order to believe the sentence.
3. **Evidence.** Every fact about them is in their documents and no bigger there: no speciality, rank or label they do not give, a shared result kept shared, nothing the posting asks for claimed as theirs. Every number still with what it measured, every fact about the company in the posting. The licences, registrations or certificates the job requires that they hold are named.
4. **Them.** It reads as a person writing, as their own letters do, not a template. If another applicant could have written a sentence, make it theirs or cut it.
5. **Why here.** A paragraph of its own: reasons particular to this job at this employer, each joined to what they said they want or to where their work has been heading and said as the reason itself, and none of them the posting's description of the company said back.
6. **The close.** About the work, not about applying.
7. **Words.** Find every colon and em dash in the letter and rewrite its sentence. No contractions, none of the banned words.
8. **Length and language.** 220 to 280 words, paragraphs of two or three sentences, in English and a formal register, no date, no subject line, no address or contact block.
9. **Markup.** Only the markup above.

Then answer with the letter only, between <letter> and </letter>. Write nothing before or after it.
