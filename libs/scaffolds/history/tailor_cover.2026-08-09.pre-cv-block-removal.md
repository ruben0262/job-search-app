MOST COMMON FAILURES — a cover letter is binned in seconds if it does these. Do NOT:
1. Open by restating the posting back at them ("Amberelectric is looking for a Full Stack
   Engineer to…", "You're hiring a…", "I am writing to apply for…"). They wrote the job.
2. Open with a clever "insight about the company" essay — trying to sound sharp reads as AI trying
   too hard, and it is not needed.
3. TELL them your experience matches, or quote their requirements back to compare it ("you need X,
   and I have done X", "this is exactly what your role requires"). That sounds like arguing the fit
   on paper. SHOW instead: describe the concrete work, and a short story, and let its relevance be
   obvious. Trust the reader to connect it — showing, not telling, is what qualifies you, in a cover
   letter as in an interview.
4. Use em-dashes (—) or colons (:) anywhere in the prose. Both are top AI tells. Humans writing a
   letter almost never reach for either. The colon habit is the one to kill, that pattern of a
   claim, then a colon, then the elaboration. Rewrite it as one plain sentence, split it into two,
   or use a comma. Real examples and their fixes:
     BAD  "What draws me to Bending Spoons is the way you hire: on general engineering ability."
     GOOD "What draws me to Bending Spoons is that you hire on general engineering ability."
     BAD  "...closer to how a day there actually goes than most graduate roles are: incomplete
           information, a deadline, and a decision to make."
     GOOD "...closer to how a day there actually goes than most graduate roles are. Incomplete
           information, a deadline, and a decision to make."
     BAD  "I think about AI work at that level by default: what API surface other teams build on."
     GOOD "I think about AI work at that level by default, down to what API surface other teams
           build on."
   The ONLY colon allowed anywhere in the document is in the contact line (email and phone).
   Zero colons in the letter body. Zero em-dashes.
5. Use contractions. Write "I have" not "I've", "I am" not "I'm", "do not" not "don't". The small
   formality reads noticeably more professional.
6. Write long, dense paragraphs. Readers skim and attention is short — keep every paragraph to 2–3
   short sentences.
7. Close weakly — generic filler ("I'd like to discuss how I can contribute", "thank you for your
   consideration") or a trail-off. End STRONG (see STRUCTURE).
8. Sound like an AI wrote it — evenly hedged, hollow, or straining to be clever. It must read as a
   professional, genuine, honest human wrote it.

Qualify the candidate the way a strong interview answer does: by SHOWING, not telling. Two things win
together — (a) it is ATS-friendly and the concrete work they describe clearly covers the role's real
requirements, and (b) a human reads it, understands them, and roots for them. Be genuinely WARM: real
enthusiasm for the work and the role, an honest voice, someone the reader would want on the team —
never a cold recital and never cleverness. Give it enough room to feel human without rambling.

STRUCTURE (aim ~200–260 words, small 2–3 sentence paragraphs, easy to skim — warm but not rambling)
- Opening (2–3 sentences): lead with a concrete, relevant piece of work — the strongest thing the candidate
  has done that matters for this role, or a short grounded anecdote that demonstrates it. SHOW the
  work; do not announce that it matches. Name the company and role naturally. The relevance should
  be self-evident from what they choose to show.
- Body, FIRST paragraph (2–4 sentences): the single most relevant thing they have built for this role,
  shown through what it did and what it achieved (include one concrete number). Let the fit speak
  for itself. Do not restate their requirements or claim the match.
  Write it in your own words, not the CV's. The CV tells you WHAT they did; this paragraph is you
  explaining it to somebody. If a phrase in this paragraph also appears in their CV, rewrite it.
  None of these belong in it: "institutional-quality", "end-to-end", "leveraging", "robust",
  "passionate", "results-driven", "dynamic", "innovative". They describe nothing — every one of
  them is true of every candidate, so a reader skips them and learns less than if the sentence
  were shorter.
- Body, SECOND paragraph (2–4 sentences): THE MOMENT. This paragraph is not a second summary of
  their experience and it is not optional. It is ONE specific occasion, told the way a person tells
  a colleague what happened. It must contain all four of these:
    (a) WHEN or WHERE it happened, concretely enough to place it ("last winter", "three days before
        the demo", "on the VPS at two in the morning").
    (b) WHAT WENT WRONG, or what was unknown, or why the thing did not exist yet.
    (c) WHAT THEY DID about it, in plain words, as an action they took.
    (d) HOW IT TURNED OUT, in one clause. It does not have to be a triumph.
  Write it in the past tense, about one occasion, not about a habit. "I once spent three days
  tracing a pricing feed that went stale every Tuesday, and the answer was a cache nobody owned"
  is the shape. "I have experience debugging complex production issues" is the failure.
  The detail is what makes it land, so include one small thing only someone who was there would
  mention. It must stay consistent with their real experience, stack and projects.
  WHAT THE MOMENT MAY AND MAY NOT BE MADE OF. The specificity has to come from narrative, never
  from new facts. You MAY add: when it happened, how long it took, the order things happened in,
  what they tried first, how it felt, what they did about it. You MAY NOT add ANY of the
  following, because the candidate would have to defend it in an interview and could not:
    - A number that is not already in their facts. No error rates, no percentages, no latencies,
      no throughputs, no user counts, no team sizes, no uptime figures. If the moment seems to
      need a number, tell it without one — "the success rate climbed" is fine, "the success rate
      climbed from 40% to 98%" is a fabrication unless both figures are theirs.
    - A named third party they have not worked with: no client, customer, vendor, provider or
      partner that is not in their facts.
    - A restatement of one of their real numbers as a DIFFERENT kind of measurement. "10k+ records
      in under 1ms" is a latency. Writing it as "ingests 10,000+ records per second" invents a
      throughput they never claimed. Keep every figure attached to the thing it actually measured.
    - A vague scale claim standing in for a number: "thousands of customers", "millions of rows",
      "a large team". If the scale is not in their facts, leave the scale out.
- Close (1–2 sentences): end STRONG and specific — a confident, forward-looking line that leaves a
  clear impression and invites a conversation.
  Write it about the WORK, never about the process of applying. Something they want to build,
  solve or see inside that company. These are the closes that waste the last thing the reader
  reads, and none of them may appear: "I am writing to express", "I believe I would be a great
  fit", "I would like to discuss how I can contribute", "I would welcome the opportunity", "I look
  forward to hearing from you", "thank you for your consideration", "I am comfortable [doing X]".
  Each one could end anybody's letter, which is what makes it worth nothing here.

THE HUMAN TOUCH — this is what's usually missing, so get it right
- Write in the candidate's own voice: professional, confident, specific, genuine and WARM — a real person
  who is genuinely keen on THIS job, not someone spraying letters.
- A short anecdote goes a long way: one concrete moment (a production bug they chased down, a tricky
  integration, a performance problem they fixed, why they built a project) earns trust and gets the
  reader reasoning in their favour. One or two sentences, specific and BELIEVABLE. It may be a
  realistic moment even if it isn't spelled out in the CV — the work they describe is real, so an incident of
  that kind almost certainly happened — as long as it is fully consistent with their real
  experience, stack and projects (the kind of thing that genuinely occurs in that work, so they could
  speak to it naturally in an interview). Keep it grounded and defensible; never a claim they couldn't
  stand behind.
- Let the fit EMERGE from what they show: choose the work whose relevance is obvious for this role,
  so the reader connects it themselves. Do not spell out the match or quote their requirements back
  — picking the right thing to show IS the connection.
- It must feel like a letter written to a person, not a form.
