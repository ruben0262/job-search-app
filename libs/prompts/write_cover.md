## Your task

Write this person's cover letter for this job: a short letter, written to a person, that makes the reader want to meet them. Their CV proves what they have done. The letter does what a CV cannot: it says why they want this job with this employer, and why they would be good at it, in their own voice. The reader gives it under a minute and is asking three things: why us, why this work, and why you. Answer those plainly and leave the proof and the detail to the CV: the letter is there to make the reader want to meet them, and to open the CV on the way.

Their own cover letters above show how they write. Keep their voice: how formal they are, and how they put things. Most people's letters are weak, generic and full of stock phrases, so keep their voice and leave those phrases behind. Use a true detail from them when it fits this job, but never copy paragraphs written for another job or company. Their CVs and other documents are the record of what they have done.

We add their name and contact details and the design. You write the letter, from the greeting to the signature, in the markup shown further down. Give it no subject line, no address block and no date.

## Plan before you write

Work through these in your thinking first:

1. List the three or four things this employer needs most, in the posting's own words, with any licence, registration or certificate it requires, and note what would make the reader hesitate about this person, such as a move or a requirement their documents do not show.
2. Find why they want this job. What they want from work is in their own cover letters and in what they told us about themselves, often said vaguely and for another employer: what they enjoy in the work, what they want more or less of, the kind of place they want, what they hope to learn or move up to. Where their documents show it, add the direction their work has been taking: what they have moved towards, job after job. Quote those lines. Where they have said little, reason it out as someone who knows them would: list the reasons a person with this record could have for wanting this job (the step it is for them, the work or the people it means more of, what this employer does and for whom, what it would teach them), and keep the one or two that are most believable for them and that a hiring manager most wants to hear. Then find two or three things about this job and this employer that give them that, each particular to the work there: what the employer does and whom it serves, what they would work on or with, how the team works, or what it offers someone in their position, such as training, a qualification or a way up. Not its awards or slogans, nothing any employer could offer, such as pay, holidays or a friendly team, and nothing that is only about what suits their own life, such as the hours or the journey.
3. Choose their case: the one thing about them that matters most for what this employer needs, a strength, a way of working or a piece of knowledge, and the one or two pieces of work that show it best, each with what came of it. A second thing only where this job plainly needs it. Quote the line in their documents each comes from. A need their documents do not answer is left alone.

## Rules

### 1. Only what is true

- Every employer, job, date, qualification, tool, skill and number comes from their documents. Never invent or estimate one, and keep each number with what it measured.
- Say each thing exactly as strongly as their documents do. No speciality, rank or label they do not give themselves, such as specialist, main, core, expert or senior; no part of a list as all of it; a result they had with others kept as shared; and nothing the posting asks for claimed as theirs. Why they want this job is the one thing you may reason out (the plan's second step): a reason is never a fact about their past, so it brings no employer, event, feeling or history they did not give us.
- Say about the company only what the posting and the note about the company say. Never invent its products, values, news or people.
- Tell their work in your own words rather than repeating the CV's lines, but add no facts: no new numbers, clients, dates or events.

### 2. What the letter says

- **Greeting:** "Dear" and the name of the person hiring, if the posting gives one; otherwise "Dear Hiring Manager,".
- **Opening, two or three sentences:** say why they want this job, then give the one fact about them that makes the reader go on. The reason comes first, as something true and particular about the work there and what it means to them. Begin it as this person would, with what they like in the work, with where their work has been going or with what this job would let them do. "I want" is one way to begin and not the way every letter begins. Then who they are for this job, in a line: what they do, where and for how long, or the result that best stands for them. Name the company and the job inside the sentence that gives the reason, or in a second sentence that goes on to say what this job would let them do. Never a sentence whose only work is to say that they are applying or to point back at the reason ("I am applying for the … role", "That is why I am interested in…", "That is what interests me about…"). The company as people say it, without a legal form, "Group" or a slogan ("Bosch", not "Bosch Group" or "Robert Bosch GmbH"). Where the job is at a place with a name of its own, a home, a hotel or a site, name the company as well as the place ("Rushcliffe Care's Beaumanor home"). When a recruiter wrote the posting for a client of theirs, the job is not at the recruiter, here or anywhere else in the letter: say the recruiter is hiring for it ("the … role that Radley James is recruiting for"), and say of the client only what kind of organisation the posting says it is, in a few plain words and without the posting's praise of it ("for a logistics client", not "at a leading global logistics provider"). The job by the title the posting gives it, as it reads in a sentence, without hours, contract, place or "m/f/d", and in the language the posting writes it in, even when that is not the letter's. Leave the board's punctuation out of it. Where a board strung keywords after the job's name, give the name alone ("Go Developer" for "Golang | Go Developer | Python | Cloud"), and write a comma for a dash and "and" for "&" ("Software Engineer, Python and Infrastructure Automation"). Put that title in `<strong>`, here and wherever else the letter names it, so the reader sees at once which job the letter is for. Never open with the application itself, in any words ("I am writing to apply", "I would like to be considered for"), never restate the posting back to the people who wrote it ("You are looking for…"), never open with an essay about the company, and never with a feeling any applicant could claim ("I was delighted to see…"). Where they live and whether they would move come later in the letter, never in the opening.
- **Their case, one paragraph of at most four sentences:** the paragraph has one idea, the thing you chose, and its first sentence says it in this person's own words. Then come the one or two pieces of work that show it, each with what came of it, so that the reader sees the person at work. Three results in a row are a CV. It is not a walk through the posting's requirements and not their CV in sentences: choose, and leave the rest to the CV. Say once what they would do with it in this job, as one particular thing they intend to do there, told in words about that work, never as a claim that their past work is this work and never as "the same care", "that same attention" or "the same approach" carried over. Tell the work itself, in words a reader of this posting will know at once as that part of the job, plain words of your own rather than the posting's phrases. Join the two with no sentence about what the job asks ("Your role asks for…", "This role needs…"), and no conclusion their documents do not state ("so I know how to…", "which is what I do every day", "I am used to…", "…is familiar to me"). The fact, well chosen and plainly named, is the connection. Keep the figure that shows the size of each piece of work where their documents give one, and leave the rest to the CV: a run of tools, versions, settings and further figures buries the claim it was meant to prove. Name a tool only where the posting names it, and no more than two in the whole letter. Never announce the match as a formula ("That is the same problem your role describes", "You need X, and I have done X", "This is exactly what you ask for"), never quote the posting's requirements back to tick them off, and never call their work the same as this job's when it is not. Name the licences, registrations or certificates this job requires that they hold. Settle what would make the reader hesitate only where their documents hold the answer, as the fact that answers it, in one plain sentence: a move they plan, a registration under way, the shifts they already work. A move is said for this job ("I would move to Oxford for this job"), never held back for "the right job". Never name what they have not done, do not hold or find hard ("I have not yet…", "…is new to me", "…takes me longer"), never raise a gap the posting does not ask about, never describe their present job as something they want out of, and never state a wish this job cannot meet, such as another place or other hours. What they lack the CV will show. The letter makes their case.
- **Why this job, here, a paragraph of its own, two to four sentences:** this is the heart of the letter. The things you found, each joined to what they said they want or to where their work has been heading, in plain words of their own rather than the posting's: what they would work on or who they would serve there, how the team works, what it would let them learn or become. Bring in one or two true things about this employer, what it does or whom it serves, as part of the reason. Say the reason itself, as a fact about the work there and what it gives them. Wanting is said plainly ("I want"), in whole sentences, and never dressed up. Never say the posting's list of duties back as the reason, and never tell the reader what their own job or company is ("This role is…", "The posting describes…", "The resort has three hotels…"). What is true of the job is said as what this person wants from it or would do in it. Leave out anything they want that this job does not offer, and any wish for a different job from this one, such as a promotion out of it, unless the posting itself names that step. What the company says of itself is a reason only when it is joined to them, never a description said back or a compliment, and never claim a history with it that their documents do not give, such as having long admired it or used its products.
- **Close, two sentences:** first a plain sentence that begins "I want" or "I would like" and names the result they want to bring about there, confident and about the work and never a timetable of their first weeks ("I want every reading group at the branch full again by spring"), then one plain line that asks to talk, about one particular thing in the work there that they would want to hear about or show, named, and never about "the role" or "the opportunity" in general. Never a stock form ("Thank you for your consideration", "I look forward to hearing from you at your earliest convenience"). Then the sign-off ("Kind regards,") and their name.
- **Length:** 220 to 280 words, in paragraphs of two or three sentences, on one page of A4. At least half of it is why they want this job and what they mean to do in it, and no more than half is what they have done.

### 3. How it is written

- **Language:** write in English, whatever language the posting and their documents are in, in the register of a careful letter to someone they have not met, polite and natural, neither stiff nor chatty. Spell as the posting does, British or American, and British when the posting is not in English.
- **Voice:** warm, plain and specific, like a capable person writing to someone they would like to work with. Concrete beats impressive. It is this person talking, in the words their own letter and what they told us use for their work and for what they want, where those are plain and true, and never in their CV's lists. What they want and what they would do is said plainly ("I want to", "I would"), never hedged ("I would want to", "I'd want to", "I would hope to"), never told by what it is not ("not just tickets", "rather than a queue of tickets"), and never set beside the job as a perfect match ("…and this role offers exactly that").
- **Flow:** it reads as one piece, as the best letter this person could write would. One line of thought runs through it, from why they want this job, through what they have done, to the result they want to bring about there, and the close comes back to the reason the letter opened on. Each paragraph does one job and begins with a sentence that leads into it from the paragraph before. Each sentence follows from the one before it, so that no fact stands alone, and two facts that belong together share a sentence. Sentences are of a natural length, none so long that it has to be read twice, and they do not all begin with "I" and a verb. The practical things, a move, a licence or the shifts they can work, are said once, in a sentence that closes the paragraph they belong to, joined to what it says. They are never a paragraph of their own, and never dropped between two sentences about something else. The names these instructions give the letter's parts are yours and never the letter's, so lead into a paragraph in words this person would use.
- Show a quality by what they did, never claim it ("I am good with people"), and never complain about a past job or employer.
- Contractions as this person writes. Where their own letter and what they told us use "I've" and "don't", so does this letter, in the same measure. Where they write words out in full, or gave us no writing of their own, so does this letter.
- No colons and no em dashes (—) in sentences: they make a letter read as generated. Rewrite as one plain sentence, or two. "The problem was simple to state: a prompt change could break another case" becomes "The problem was simple to state. A prompt change could fix one case and quietly break another." A list after a colon becomes a sentence: "three services, a scraper, a processor and a scorer".
- Never use these words or phrases, which describe nothing: passionate, results-driven, dynamic, motivated, team player, hard-working, proven track record, leverage, synergy, cutting-edge, seamless, robust, innovative, thrilled, end-to-end, "I am excited to", "I believe I would be a great fit", "the job description".

## Final check

Before you answer, read your letter as the hiring manager will, and fix whatever fails:

1. **The opening.** It says why they want this job, in a way no other job would fit, gives the one fact about them that makes the reader go on, and names the company as people say it and the job by the posting's own title, in `<strong>`. It is not "I am writing to", does not restate the posting, is not about the company, and no sentence in the letter only says that they are applying.
2. **Three questions.** A reader who has only this letter knows why this employer, why this work and why this person. Their case is one idea, said first and shown by one or two pieces of work they did, in words that reader knows as their own job, and it is not their CV in sentences. No sentence says what the job asks, announces the match or draws a conclusion their documents do not state. Each piece of work keeps the one figure that shows its size, and no tool, version or setting stays that the reader does not need in order to believe the sentence.
3. **Evidence.** Every fact about them is in their documents and no bigger there: no speciality, rank or label they do not give, a shared result kept shared, nothing the posting asks for claimed as theirs. Every number still with what it measured, every fact about the company in the posting or the note about it. A reason for wanting the job may be reasoned out; a fact about them may not. The licences, registrations or certificates the job requires that they hold are named.
4. **Them, and the flow.** It reads as a person writing, as their own letters do, not a template. If another applicant could have written a sentence, make it theirs or cut it. No sentence is built as "What I … is …" or "What draws me to … is …": say it straight ("I want…", "I like…"). None carries "the same care" or "the same approach" over. Then read it through once as the reader will. Wherever a sentence does not follow from the one before it, a fact stands alone or a paragraph begins without leading on from the last, rewrite it so that it does.
5. **Why here.** A paragraph of its own: reasons particular to this job at this employer, each joined to what they said they want or to where their work has been heading and said as the reason itself, and none of them the posting's description of the company said back.
6. **The close.** Two sentences. A plain sentence on the result they want to bring about there, then one plain line asking to talk, and no stock form.
7. **Words.** Find every colon and em dash in the letter and rewrite its sentence. Count the tools, products and standards the letter names. Where there are more than two, keep the two this posting names and tell the rest as the work it was. Contractions only as their own writing has them, no "would want to", none of the banned words.
8. **Length and language.** 220 to 280 words, counted, in paragraphs of two or three sentences, in English as a careful letter is written, no date, no subject line, no address or contact block.
9. **Markup.** Only the markup above.

Then answer with the letter only, between <letter> and </letter>. Write nothing before or after it.
